VAT is already a behavioural instrument — it makes things more or less expensive. Applying it with environmental intent is not a new idea; it is an extension of what VAT already does.

The polluting products that carry higher rates:

<table class="rd-fiscal-table">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Product</th><th>New VAT rate</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Single-use plastic (bottles, bags, cutlery, packaging)</td><td>40%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>New petrol and diesel vehicles</td><td>30%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>New gas boilers</td><td>30%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Patio heaters and petrol garden machinery</td><td>30%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Private jets, helicopters, superyachts</td><td>30%</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

These are not bans. A gas boiler is still available. It simply carries a VAT rate that reflects the cost it imposes on the transition — and makes the heat pump look considerably more attractive at the point of purchase.

## Bring Back the Milk Bottle

The most innovative element is the reverse VAT: a 5% government subsidy on products sold in certified returnable packaging, paid at the point of sale. The customer sees a lower price on the shelf. No moralising required.

The milk bottle isn't just nostalgia. It is a working model of circular economy infrastructure: durable container, deposit mechanism, return infrastructure, industrial cleaning, repeat. The economics worked because the deposit created an incentive to return, the return created a supply of clean empties, and the clean empties made the whole system cheaper than a linear alternative.

What broke the milk bottle was the supermarket and the plastic bottle: convenience, combined with artificially cheap single-use packaging whose disposal cost was externalised. The reverse VAT corrects that distortion.

Three examples of how it works at the shelf:

<table class="rd-fiscal-table">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Product</th><th>Customer pays</th><th>vs standard price</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Milk in glass bottle (£1.50) + 20p deposit</td><td>£1.43 + 20p</td><td>7p cheaper</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Shampoo in refillable container (£4) + 50p deposit</td><td>£3.80 + 50p</td><td>20p cheaper</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Takeaway in returnable container (£8) + £1 deposit</td><td>£7.60 + £1</td><td>40p cheaper</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Qualifying packaging must be part of a verified return scheme with a 95%+ return rate, industrial sterilisation, and tracking to food safety standards. The subsidy is capped at £250m per year; if uptake exceeds the cap, the per-unit subsidy reduces proportionally.
