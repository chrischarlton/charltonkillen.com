The built environment is one of the clearest places where the current system's incentives produce the wrong outcomes at scale. Not because developers are malicious, not because planners are incompetent, but because the tax system makes sprawl cheap and density expensive.

Think of the typical out-of-town retail park: a sprawling single-storey building surrounded by acres of tarmac parking. You get there easily enough, park up, then face a long walk to reach the entrance. Nearby, the town centre has struggled for years, unable to compete with the perceived ease of big-box retail and online commerce. The communities around those town centres have also struggled.

Business Rates are the mechanism that produces this outcome. Land-hungry out-of-town developments incur very low rates. Efficient multi-storey buildings in town centres are taxed more heavily. It is exactly the wrong way round.

## The Ikea principle

Ikea in Ashton-under-Lyne is the proof of concept. Built on a more constrained town centre site across multiple levels, with parking integrated into the building itself — including the roof — it is self-evidently more efficient and a better use of land. Yet it is not the default for out-of-town sites, because in the current system it simply makes more sense to spread across four acres than to compress into one.

If we have permission to build on four acres for a shopping centre and its car park, why would we compress into one acre with more floors when the system doesn't reward us for doing so? We could use the remaining three acres for housing, even while saving some for nature. But we don't, because the incentive isn't there.

The Land Value Tax creates that incentive. A development on four acres pays four times the LVT of a one-acre equivalent. Suddenly compression makes financial sense. The Ikea solution becomes the rational choice, not the unusual one.

## Housing density follows the same logic

Housing developers follow incentives too. Land banking — holding undeveloped land until the optimal moment — is rational under the current system. When holding idle land is free, there is no cost to waiting. Development that does take place is often slow and at low density, maintaining the scarcity that keeps prices high.

The LVT makes holding land expensive. The rational response is to build. And when building becomes more urgent, density increases — more homes on the same footprint, more people within walking distance of the town centre, more vitality in the communities that have been quietly hollowing out for decades.

Warrington is the setting for [Chapter 6 of the book](/redirected/book/): a place that benefits from these reforms. Employment costs fall, businesses reinvest, a nurse who left for London considers coming back because it makes economic sense to. The value created by a community stays with that community.

When a new tram line opens, local land values rise. Under the current system, that gain goes entirely to whoever owns the land. Under the LVT, rising land values mean rising tax revenue - returned to the community that created that value through its own collective investment. It is not redistribution. It is correction.
