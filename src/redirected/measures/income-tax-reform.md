All income is taxed the same way, under four bands:

<table class="rd-fiscal-table">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Income</th><th>Rate</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>£0 – £25,000</td><td>0%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>£25,001 – £100,000</td><td>25%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>£100,001 – £250,000</td><td>42%</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Over £250,000</td><td>47%</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Employment, self-employment, dividends, capital gains, interest, rental income, pension drawdowns are all taxed identically under these bands. The separate Capital Gains Tax system (18%/24% on property, 10%/20% on other assets), the separate dividend tax system (8.75% to 39.35%), and the separate interest tax system are abolished.

The current tax system treats money differently depending on where it comes from. Earn £50k working and you pay income tax at standard rates. Receive £50k in dividends and you pay a lower rate. Sell assets for a £50k gain and you pay capital gains tax — lower still, with an annual exempt amount on top.

There is no economic justification for this. The argument that capital must be taxed lightly to encourage investment is an assertion dressed as an axiom. The UK has had preferentially low capital taxes for decades. The result is not a nation of bold investors; it is a nation of property landlords and dividend-sheltering contractors.

This measure does not punish investment. It simply ends the legal fiction that money earned from owning things is fundamentally different from money earned from doing things.
